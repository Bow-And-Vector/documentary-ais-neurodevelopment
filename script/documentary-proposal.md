# Documentary proposal: The Binary Myth

## Working title
The Binary Myth

## Documentary type
Feature documentary / investigative public-interest documentary

## Length
80–100 minutes

## Format
Narrative documentary blending scientific explanation, lived experience, policy analysis, and visual storytelling.

## Logline
A documentary examining prenatal sex development, androgen insensitivity syndrome, and the science of brain development asks a simple but radical question: what happens when the human body does not fit the binary categories our culture insists on—and why are those assumptions now shaping medical policy for children in Texas and beyond?

## Synopsis
From fetal development to public policy, this documentary follows the science of androgen signaling, receptor variation, and prenatal hormone exposure to explore why sex, identity, and embodiment are far more complex than a simple male/female binary. Using the lens of androgen insensitivity syndrome—especially partial and mild forms—the film shows how biological variation can produce bodies and neural patterns that do not fit clean categories.

As the story moves from the laboratory to the lived experience of individuals with developmental variation, it reveals the human cost of treating sex as a fixed and simplistic concept. In Texas and across the United States, the documentary argues, policymakers are restricting care for gender-diverse youth by relying on outdated assumptions about biology that do not reflect current understanding of developmental science.

## Core premise
The documentary argues that developmental biology, especially in the context of AIS and related conditions, demonstrates that the human body is not neatly reducible to a simple male/female binary—and that policy built on this false simplicity can endanger real people.

## Why this story matters now
The film emerges from a present in which the politics of sex and gender are accelerating in state legislatures, school boards, courts, and the media. Texas has enacted restrictions on medical treatment for gender-diverse youth under the conviction that sex is fixed, binary, and obvious. But the actual science of development is far more complex, and that complexity matters when the lives of children and families are at stake.

The documentary does not simply oppose those policies. It asks a deeper question: what does it mean to tell the public that biology is simple when the science of development is not?

## Intended audience
- general documentary viewers
- students of biology, medicine, and public policy
- medical practitioners and policy researchers
- families affected by developmental variation
- people interested in sex, gender, scientific literacy, and public discourse

## Visual language
The film balances rigorous science with emotional testimony. It uses:

- animated explanations of embryology and hormone signaling
- clean diagrams of receptor function and genital development
- intimate portraits and personal narratives
- policy footage and legal timeline sequences
- measured, thoughtful visual design that respects the seriousness of the topic

## Key themes
- the complexity of sex differentiation
- varying androgen receptor function and developmental outcomes
- prenatal hormone effects and the developing brain
- lived experience in a culture that demands binary categories
- the policy consequences of biological simplification
- the need for scientific nuance in public life

## Key questions the film asks
- What is sex development, and how is it shaped by timing and receptors?
- What does AIS reveal about the limits of a fixed binary model?
- How does developmental biology complicate our assumptions about gender and embodiment?
- What do we know about prenatal hormone effects on the brain—and what do we not know?
- Why do simplistic claims about sex persist in public policy?
- What are the consequences of those claims for young people and their families?

## Documentary structure

### Act I — The body as a process
Establish the idea that sex development is not a simple binary but a biological process shaped by context, timing, and receptor function.

### Act II — AIS and developmental variation
Introduce AIS as a case study in how the human body can vary under conditions of altered androgen signaling and receptor functionality.

### Act III — The developing brain
Discuss prenatal hormone exposure and brain development with precision, distinguishing between evidence and oversimplification.

### Act IV — Human experience
Center the lived experience of people whose bodies and lives challenge social assumptions about sex and gender.

### Act V — The policy front
Move into Texas and the wider US policy landscape to show how simple biological narratives are being used to justify restrictions on medical care.

### Act VI — What a scientific society could look like
Offer a conclusion rooted in dignity, nuance, and a more accurate understanding of human development.

## Production notes
This is a documentary about both science and public life. The film should avoid either side falling into abstraction. The science should remain precise, and the policy analysis should remain grounded in real-world consequences for people and families.

This project is positioned as a serious, rigorous documentary that treats both scientific evidence and human experience with care. It is designed to be educational, emotionally resonant, and politically urgent without sacrificing intellectual honesty.
