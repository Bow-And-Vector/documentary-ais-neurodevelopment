# Documentary pitch: "The Binary Myth"

## Logline

A documentary about the science of sex development, prenatal hormone exposure, and androgen insensitivity syndrome reveals how deeply narrow our ideas of male and female are—and how those simplified beliefs are being used to justify medical restrictions on gender-diverse youth in Texas and beyond.

## Short synopsis

At the intersection of developmental biology, neuroscience, and public policy, this documentary follows the science of androgen signaling, receptor variation, and prenatal brain development to ask why our culture still treats sex as if it were a simple binary. Using the framework of androgen insensitivity syndrome—especially partial and mild forms—the film shows how bodies and brains can develop in ways that challenge simplistic assumptions about masculinity and femininity.

As the story moves from the laboratory to the lived experience of individuals with developmental variation, it reveals how public policy in Texas and elsewhere is being built on a false idea of biological certainty. The film argues that the medical restrictions imposed on gender-diverse youth are not only harmful—they are based on an outdated and incomplete understanding of human development.

## Extended synopsis

The film opens with the basic biology of sexual differentiation and quickly broadens into a question that is both scientific and social: what counts as a male body, a female body, or a brain shaped by hormones during development? Using AIS as a case study, the story explores how the androgen receptor system can produce a range of developmental outcomes that do not align neatly with simplistic categories.

From there, the film travels into neuroscience, where prenatal hormone exposure, neural organization, and receptor sensitivity are discussed with the complexity they deserve. The science does not support simplistic claims that a person’s identity can be inferred from a binary body map. But it does support something more important: the human organism develops through a complex interaction of genetic, endocrine, and environmental systems, and those processes vary.

The middle of the film centers lived experience. Interviews with people with AIS and related developmental contexts complicate public assumptions and show the emotional and medical consequences of being treated as if difference is illegitimate. The documentary humanizes the science without erasing its complexity.

The final act turns toward Texas and the national policy landscape: a place where lawmakers and medical boards are restricting care for gender-diverse minors under the belief that sex is only two categories and that medicine is best governed by simplistic binary frameworks. The film argues that these restrictions ignore the science of endocrine variation and can increase harm for young people whose bodies and developmental pathways do not fit the ideological frame imposed on them.

The closing message is urgent and humane: if society is to have a scientifically honest conversation about sex, gender, and medical care, it must leave behind the false promise of a tidy biological binary.

## Target audience

- general documentary audiences
- students of science, medicine, and public policy
- families and clinicians navigating sex development questions
- viewers interested in gender, identity, and bodily autonomy
- viewers concerned with medical misinformation and lawmaking

## Documentary tone

- informed and rigorous
- compassionate and human-centered
- politically urgent but not polemical
- visually compelling
- grounded in lived experience and scientific complexity

## Visual and editorial approach

- animation of fetal development and endocrine signaling
- clean diagrams showing androgen receptor function
- macro views of the body and neural systems without sensationalizing
- intimate portraiture and testimony from people with lived experience
- timelines and maps of Texas legislative action and policy changes
- explanatory sections that keep science understandable for a broad audience

## Core argument in one sentence

The film argues that developmental biology, especially in AIS and related conditions, proves that sex and human variation are more complex than rigid binary frameworks—and that this complexity matters in the real-life policy decisions affecting young people today.
