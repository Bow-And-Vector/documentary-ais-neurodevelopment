## SEGMENT 1: WHO YOU ARE (2 min)

**Setup:**
You, looking directly at camera. Calm. Clear. Speaking to Texas voters.

**Script:**

"My name is [name]. I'm from College Station, Texas—home of Texas A&M, where my father taught and researched. I'm [age] years old.

And I want to tell you something that the people running Texas right now don't want you to know: I have a mosaic brain.

When I was born, I had hypospadias—a developmental variation. It's not a disease. It's not a disorder. It's a variation in how the body develops in the womb.

But here's what matters: if you look at the science of human development, hypospadias tells us something important. It tells us that sex development is not a simple binary switch. It's a process. A complex, chemical, hormonally-driven process shaped by timing and variation.

For most of my life, I didn't understand what that meant. I didn't understand what it meant about my brain, about my body, about who I am.

I grew up Christian, in a conservative Texas community. I was taught—and I believed—that God made people male or female. That's it. Binary. Obvious. End of story.

But that story wasn't true. At least not for me."

## SEGMENT 2: THE DISCOVERY (3 min)

**Setup:**
Still direct to camera. This gets more personal. Let emotion show.

**Script:**

"When I was in my twenties, I got sick. Lyme disease. Mold exposure. POTS—postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome. My body couldn't regulate blood pressure when I stood up.

My doctor advised me to wear compression stockings. Tight stockings that would help push blood back to my heart.

One day, I went to buy them. The sales lady recommended women's compression stockings instead of men's. They were cheaper, and they fit better.

I bought them. I went home. I put them on.

And when I looked in the mirror...

[Pause. This is important. Let the camera sit on your face for a moment.]

...something in my brain lit up.

It wasn't a choice. It wasn't something I decided to feel. It was a response—neurological, chemical, real—to a stimulus. A dopamine response. My nervous system activated something that had been latent, dormant, not expressed.

I felt *female*. And it felt *right*.

That feeling didn't make sense to me at the time. I pushed it away. I went back to living as I had been.

But it didn't go away. For ten years, it didn't go away.

So I started exploring it. Privately at first. I'd wear women's clothes, women's makeup, women's presentation. And every time, that same dopamine response. That same sense of *rightness*.

Eventually, I did it in public too. I let myself be seen that way. Not constantly. But increasingly.

I was discovering my own neurobiology. My own brain. The part of me that my biology had made possible.

That's not ideology. That's neuroscience. That's my nervous system telling me something true about myself."

## SEGMENT 3: THE COLLISION (2 min)

**Setup:**
Direct to camera. This is harder. You're describing pain and rejection. Let that be real.

**Script:**

"Eventually, I told my family.

My family is smart. They're scientists. My father did research at Texas A&M. I thought—I hoped—that they would be open to understanding something new. Something that science was showing them about their own child.

They weren't.

They told me I would not be welcome at family gatherings if I dressed that way. They couldn't accept it. They wouldn't accept it.

And here's what I learned from that: it's not about intelligence. My family is intelligent. It's not about love. They love me.

It's about something deeper. It's about a belief system so rigid, so tied to identity, that even when confronted with evidence—real evidence from their own child—they can't integrate it.

They can't change their minds.

That's the Semmelweis problem. Doctors refused to wash their hands, refused to accept that *they* were killing patients, even when the evidence was undeniable. Why? Because admitting the truth would mean admitting they'd caused harm. And their brains couldn't do that. Their sense of self, their defensiveness, was stronger than their commitment to evidence.

My family couldn't accept what I was telling them about myself because it contradicted their worldview. And revising that worldview would cost them something—cognitively, emotionally, spiritually.

So they chose to reject me instead of revise their beliefs.

That's a neurobiological problem. That's a problem with how human brains work under threat.

And right now, that same problem is being written into law in Texas."

## SEGMENT 4: WHY THIS MATTERS NOW (3 min)

**Setup:**
Direct to camera. This is your call to action. You're speaking to voters now.

**Script:**

"In Texas, where I'm from, the government has made it illegal for doctors to provide gender-affirming medical care to kids under eighteen.

Senate Bill 14. It's the law. It went into effect last September. And it's based on a falsehood.

The argument is simple: sex is binary. Male or female. That's all. Government shouldn't allow doctors to treat it as if it's anything else.

But that argument is not true. That argument is not science.

I'm standing here as evidence. My hypospadias. My mosaic brain. My lived experience of a neurobiological variation that my own culture told me couldn't exist.

The science is clear: prenatal androgens—hormones produced in the womb—shape not just the body but the brain. And that shaping happens on a spectrum. It's not binary. It's a complex, chemical process.

When androgen receptors don't respond normally to those hormones—which is what happens in androgen insensitivity syndrome—the result is developmental variation. Real variation. Medically meaningful variation.

And when that variation affects the brain? When it creates a neural pattern that doesn't fit the binary boxes your culture has prepared for you? That's when the trouble starts.

Because your family might reject you. Your church might reject you. And now, your government is rejecting you too.

SB 14 doesn't just restrict medical care. It criminalizes doctors for acknowledging that variation exists.

And the kids who need care? They suffer. Their families suffer. The doctors who want to help them suffer.

All because the government has decided that sex is 'incontrovertible'—that it's beyond question, beyond evidence, beyond revision.

But that's not what science is. Science questions. Science revises. Science says 'we don't know yet' and 'the evidence is more complex than we thought.'

When a government claims something is scientifically incontrovertible, you should ask: who benefits from this not being questioned?

On November 5th, Texas votes for a new governor. And on October 15th, early voting begins.

Before you cast your ballot, I want you to understand: this election is about whether Texas will acknowledge the science of human development, or whether it will continue to enforce a false certainty.

It's about whether Texas will trust doctors and families to make medical decisions, or whether the government will criminalize that trust.

It's about whether people like me—people whose brains and bodies are evidence of developmental variation—will be welcomed and cared for, or whether we'll be rejected and criminalized.

Please vote for science. Please vote for the evidence. Please vote for people.

Before early voting starts on October 15th, I want Texas voters to know: your government is lying about the biology. And real people are paying the price.

Thank you."
