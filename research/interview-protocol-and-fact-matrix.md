# Interview protocol and fact matrix

## Purpose
This document is intended to organize the documentary’s interview strategy and to ensure that every major claim in the film is supported by verified, relevant source material.

## Interview categories

### A. Scientific and medical experts
Purpose: explain how sex development works, how AIS fits into that process, and how prenatal hormone exposure can affect development without oversimplifying the science.

#### Interview 1: Pediatric endocrinologist or DSD specialist
Core questions:
- Can you explain the spectrum of AIS and why it matters medically?
- What is the clinical significance of androgen receptor variation?
- How does the medical community think about variation in sex development?
- What are the most common misunderstandings the public has about this area of biology?
- How do you distinguish between robust science and speculative interpretation?

#### Interview 2: Geneticist or molecular biologist studying the androgen receptor
Core questions:
- What does the androgen receptor do in fetal development?
- How do receptor mutations affect body development and phenotype?
- Why is AIS clinically variable rather than a single fixed condition?
- How important is understanding receptor sensitivity and developmental timing?

#### Interview 3: Developmental neuroscientist or endocrinologist studying prenatal hormone effects on the brain
Core questions:
- What does the evidence say about prenatal hormone effects on brain development?
- Why are these effects more nuanced than many public narratives suggest?
- What are the strongest and weakest parts of the available research?
- How should a lay audience understand the relationship between hormones and neural development?

### B. Lived experience experts
Purpose: humanize the argument and illustrate how developmental variation affects body image, medical care, and social misinterpretation.

#### Interview 4: Person with AIS or related developmental variation
Core questions:
- What was your diagnosis journey like?
- How did your body or development differ from the assumptions others made about you?
- What kinds of misunderstandings have you encountered in medical settings or in everyday life?
- How do you explain your experience to people who assume sex is simple or obvious?
- What aspects of your story are most important for the public to understand?

#### Interview 5: Parent or family member of someone with AIS or DSD
Core questions:
- How did you understand the diagnosis when it first emerged?
- What was the experience of getting information from doctors or the public?
- How did social assumptions shape your family’s experience?
- What did you learn about how difficult it is to talk about developmental variation?

### C. Policy and legal experts
Purpose: connect the science to Texas policy and show how biological misunderstanding becomes public treatment restrictions.

#### Interview 6: Policy analyst or legal scholar on Texas restrictions on gender-diverse care for minors
Core questions:
- What legal arguments are being made about sex and medical care in Texas?
- How are these arguments framed in relation to biology?
- What is the policy impact of reducing sex to a binary assumption?
- How do these restrictions affect clinicians and families?

#### Interview 7: Pediatrician or clinician practicing in this area
Core questions:
- What do these restrictions mean in practice for care?
- How do policy restrictions interact with medical standards and patient needs?
- What do you think the public often gets wrong about the relationship between sex, medicine, and development?

#### Interview 8: Advocacy leader or counselor working with affected youth and families
Core questions:
- What do families and clinicians experience when policy decisions rely on simplified biological claims?
- What are the real-life harms of misinformed restrictions?
- What would a more scientifically literate and humane policy look like?

## Script fact matrix

This matrix is for matching major documentary claims to evidentiary support and interview material.

### Claim 1: Human sex development is a complex process shaped by timing, hormones, and receptor function.
Evidence needed:
- endocrine review literature on sex differentiation
- developmental biology references on fetal sex development
- clinical reviews on DSD and AIS

Interview support:
- endocrinologist
- geneticist or molecular biologist

### Claim 2: AIS is a medically recognized condition with a range of severities and phenotypes.
Evidence needed:
- AIS review articles
- clinical literature on complete, partial, and mild AIS
- endocrine society guidance

Interview support:
- endocrinologist
- DSD specialist

### Claim 3: Androgen receptor dysfunction is a real and meaningful cause of variation in sex development.
Evidence needed:
- androgen receptor gene studies
- clinical genetics literature
- receptor-function reviews

Interview support:
- geneticist / molecular biologist

### Claim 4: Prenatal hormone exposure affects brain development, but the evidence is nuanced and not reducible to simplistic stereotypes.
Evidence needed:
- developmental neuroscience reviews
- prenatal hormone research
- expert interpretation of research limits

Interview support:
- neuroscientist
- endocrinologist

### Claim 5: Public policy that assumes sex is simple and binary can be medically and socially harmful.
Evidence needed:
- Texas legal documents and policy language
- medical association statements
- legal commentary and reporting on the policy impact

Interview support:
- policy analyst
- clinician
- advocate

### Claim 6: Lived experience complicates public assumptions and reveals the cost of misreading development.
Evidence needed:
- patient narrative interviews
- family accounts
- clinician commentary

Interview support:
- person with AIS
- family member
- advocate

## Interview readiness checklist

Before filming, confirm:
- subject’s expertise and role
- willingness to discuss the topic candidly
- comfort with the documentary’s framing and tone
- consent and release process
- whether they are comfortable being identified by name
- whether they are willing to be quoted on sensitive issues

## Source verification standard

Every major claim in the film should be backed by at least one of the following:
- peer-reviewed review or study
- recognized medical society guideline or clinical resource
- official legislative or legal document
- reputable reporting on a real public-policy development
- firsthand witness testimony from an interviewed subject

## Fact-checking rules

- Do not overstate brain research as proving any single identity model.
- Distinguish between developmental variation and identity categories.
- Do not present AIS as a universal explanation for any broader social claim.
- Use the language people prefer when speaking about themselves.
- Keep publicly contested claims clearly marked as such when needed.

## Documentation workflow

For each interview, record:
- name and role
- date and location
- interview type (expert / lived experience / policy)
- key themes discussed
- source material referenced by the subject
- follow-up questions
- whether the interview supports a script claim or a contextual scene

## Summary

This document ensures the film stays rigorous, compassionate, and policy-aware. The science is not merely a backdrop—it is the spine of the story. The interviews should serve as both human testimony and factual grounding, while the fact matrix keeps the film accountable to what the evidence actually supports.
