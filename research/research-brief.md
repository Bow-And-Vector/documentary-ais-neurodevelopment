# Research brief: AIS, prenatal brain development, and the Texas policy debate

## Project thesis

This documentary examines the science of developmental variation to explain why sex differentiation is not reducible to a simple male/female binary. It focuses on androgen insensitivity syndrome (AIS), particularly partial and mild forms, as a concrete example of how biological development can produce bodies and neural patterns that do not fit simplistic assumptions.

The film then connects this science to the current policy environment in Texas and nationally, where restrictions on medical care for gender-diverse youth are often justified through claims that sex is binary and fixed. The documentary argues that these policies misread developmental biology and can cause avoidable harm.

## Documentary goal

The documentary aims to:

- explain AIS and its relevance to the understanding of sex development,
- clarify what prenatal hormone exposure and androgen receptor function can and cannot tell us about brain development,
- present the lived experience of people with AIS or related developmental variation,
- show how public policy in Texas can be built on a false binary model of sex,
- challenge audiences to engage with scientific complexity without abandoning empathy or dignity.

## Central scientific points to verify

The documentary should anchor itself to these factual claims, each of which should be supported by clear, reputable sources.

1. Human sex development is a process shaped by timing, genetics, receptor function, and hormones.
2. AIS is a spectrum, not a single uniform category; partial and mild AIS are clinically recognized conditions.
3. Androgen receptor function is central to the development of male-typical anatomy in the embryo, but the biology is variable and context-dependent.
4. Prenatal hormone exposure likely affects brain development, but the evidence is nuanced and not reducible to simplistic claims.
5. The existence of developmental variation challenges rigid binary frameworks in public discourse.
6. Policy restrictions on gender-affirming care for minors are based on assumptions that do not reflect the complexity of sex development as understood in endocrinology and medical genetics.

## Key scientific themes

### 1. Sex development is a spectrum of processes, not a single switch

Human development involves multiple systems that differentiate over time:

- genetic sex
- gonadal development
- hormonal signaling
- external genital development
- internal reproductive structures
- neural organization and behavior-related pathways

Sex development is not a single event. It is a layered process shaped by timing and receptor sensitivity. This is important because policies that assume sex is always cleanly binary often ignore the real biology of variation.

### 2. AIS and receptor variation are direct examples of biological complexity

AIS occurs when androgen receptors do not respond normally to androgens. This can produce a wide range of phenotypes depending on the severity of receptor dysfunction, the residue of androgen signaling, and the developmental context.

Key subtopics:

- complete AIS
- partial AIS
- mild AIS
- androgen receptor mutations and genetic variation
- how genotype and phenotype interact in practice

This material matters because AIS is one of the clearest ways to show that developmental biology is not neatly reducible to a binary script.

### 3. Prenatal hormone exposure and the brain are real topics, but the evidence requires precision

The documentary should not claim that prenatal androgen exposure establishes a simple biological rule for identity, behavior, or a clean male/female brain division. That would overstate the evidence. Instead, the film should explain that:

- hormones influence brain development during sensitive periods,
- developmental biology is variable,
- research suggests hormonal signals matter in a complex way,
- scientific understanding remains nuanced and cautious.

This is the most important scientific distinction for the film: the evidence supports the reality of developmental influence, but not simplistic one-to-one explanations.

## Major claims to support with evidence

### Claim 1: AIS is a real, medically recognized condition with significant variability

Support with:

- clinical reviews on AIS
- endocrinology guidance on androgen receptor function
- genetics literature on receptor mutations and phenotype variability

This claim helps show that sex development is a spectrum of biological outcomes rather than a simple binary script.

### Claim 2: Androgen signaling and receptor sensitivity matter in fetal development

Support with:

- endocrine and developmental biology reviews
- literature on fetal differentiation and receptor biology
- studies of partial or mild AIS phenotypes

This claim is central for explaining how body morphology and physiology can vary without being reducible to one rigid category.

### Claim 3: Developmental biology complicates simplistic binary assumptions

Support with:

- DSD literature
- studies of androgen receptor function
- developmental neuroscience reviews

This claim sets the frame for the film: complexity is not a political abstraction but a biological reality.

### Claim 4: Prenatal hormone effects on the brain are real but complex and not fully mapped

Support with:

- neuroscience reviews on prenatal hormone exposure and brain development
- careful interpretation of sex-difference research
- literature emphasizing uncertainty and complexity

This claim allows the documentary to talk about the brain without overclaiming. It can say that the research supports developmental influence, while stressing that it does not justify simplistic claims.

### Claim 5: Texas policy is built on a simplified notion of sex and gender that does not reflect the complexity of developmental biology

Support with:

- Texas legal documents and legislative guidance
- reporting on the enactment and enforcement of SB 14
- policy analysis showing how sex is framed in law
- medical association positions on gender-affirming care for minors

This claim connects the science to public life and to the consequences of public misunderstanding.

## Interview strategy

### Scientific interviews

Priority interview targets:

- pediatric endocrinologist with experience in AIS or DSD
- geneticist or molecular biologist studying androgen receptor variation
- developmental neuroscientist who studies prenatal hormones and brain development
- clinician familiar with gender-diverse care and endocrine variation

Interview questions:

- How do clinicians think about a spectrum of AIS phenotypes?
- What do we know about androgen receptor function and developmental variability?
- What does the science actually say about prenatal hormone exposure and the brain?
- How should the public understand complexity without losing rigor?

### Human experience interviews

Priority interview targets:

- person with partial or mild AIS
- parent or family member of a person with AIS or DSD
- clinician or advocate working with affected families
- people whose experiences challenge binary assumptions about sex and identity

Interview questions:

- What was your experience of diagnosis and medical explanation?
- How has your experience of embodiment differed from social assumptions?
- What kinds of misunderstanding have you encountered in medical systems or public life?
- How does your experience relate to the broader policy debates?

### Policy interviews

Priority interview targets:

- policy analyst or legal scholar focused on Texas gender-affirming care restrictions
- clinician or pediatric specialist who has treated gender-diverse minors
- advocacy organization representative working on this issue

Interview questions:

- What do the Texas restrictions say about how sex is being defined in law?
- What are the medical implications of policies built on simple binary assumptions?
- How do these laws affect families and physicians?
- What are the consequences of ignoring developmental variation in public policy?

## Recommended overarching narrative structure

The film should move in this order:

1. Introduction: the body, the brain, and the myth of a pure binary
2. Developmental biology: how bodies form and why the process is variable
3. AIS as a real example of biological complexity
4. The brain and prenatal hormone exposure: what we know and what we do not know
5. Lived experience: stories that complicate public assumptions
6. Policy: Texas and the politics of medical restrictions
7. Conclusion: why scientific complexity matters for care, dignity, and public life

## Editing principles

- Keep the science precise, not sensational.
- Do not overstate the brain research.
- Let the viewer see the difference between evidence-based science and political simplification.
- Make room for the emotional stakes without abandoning the argument.

## Production caution

The documentary should be careful with terminology. Use both precision and respect. A few key editorial principles:

- distinguish between sex development, gender identity, and medical care frameworks,
- avoid conflating all identities with AIS or DSD,
- avoid treating medical variation as proof of a political conclusion,
- avoid arguing something that the evidence does not actually establish.

## Summary

This documentary should not argue that every person with a male body is gender-diverse, nor that every case of developmental variation maps neatly onto a particular identity. Instead, it should argue that biological reality is more complex than a simple male/female binary allows, and that when policy ignores this complexity, it does real harm.

The central story is not only about a medical condition. It is about the cost of pretending that human development is as simple as the public rhetoric that sometimes surrounds it.
